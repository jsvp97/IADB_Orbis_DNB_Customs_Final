\begin{abstract}\noindent
We study the role of multinational firms in the agricultural exports of Latin America. We combine customs records for nine countries over 2012--2018 with ownership information from Orbis and Dun \& Bradstreet to distinguish foreign multinational affiliates, domestic multinational affiliates, and local firms. Multinational affiliates account for 48 percent of agricultural exports, and this share changes little over time. Most multinational exports are shipped by foreign affiliates whose ultimate parents are concentrated in a small number of countries and large corporate groups. At the origin--destination--product--year level, exports are higher where more multinational firms are present, including exports by local firms. At the firm level, the negative association between distance and exports is smaller for multinational affiliates than for local firms, particularly when the group has an affiliate in the destination or the destination is the parent's country. These patterns are consistent with multinational networks being associated with broader market access, but the empirical relationships are correlations rather than causal effects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================
Multinational firms are at the center of international trade. About 80 percent of world trade is
linked to the international production networks of multinational firms (UNCTAD, 2013). Trade is
also concentrated in a few large firms: in a sample of 32 countries, the largest exporter accounts
for about 14 percent of non-oil exports and the five largest for about 30 percent (Freund and
Pierola, 2015). Agriculture is no exception. Four trading companies, known as the ABCD, control
over 70 percent of the global grain market (Murphy, Burch and Clapp, 2012; Clapp, 2015), and
multinational firms from developed countries lead the supplier, processing, trading and retail
stages of agricultural value chains (UNCTAD, 2009).

For Latin America, this concentration is particularly relevant. Agriculture accounts for 35 percent
of the merchandise exports of the nine countries we study, and more than half in Argentina, Paraguay
and Uruguay. Yet there is limited systematic evidence on which firms ship these exports. Customs
records identify what a country exports and where it goes, but not who owns the exporting firm.
Foreign direct investment statistics show where capital is invested, but do not link ownership to
the products and destination markets served by individual firms. Consequently, we know relatively
little about the share of the region's agricultural exports accounted for by multinational firms,
where their ultimate parents are based, and whether export patterns differ where these firms are
present.

We address three questions. First, what share of Latin America's agricultural exports is
accounted for by multinational firms? Second, where are the ultimate parents of these firms based,
and how concentrated is ownership across parent countries and corporate groups? Third, are exports
larger where multinational firms are present, and is the negative association between distance and
exports smaller for multinational affiliates than for local firms?

To answer these questions, we assemble a database linking customs records for exports from nine
Latin American countries (Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, El
Salvador, Paraguay, Peru and Uruguay) over 2012--2018 to ownership information from Orbis and Dun
\& Bradstreet. The customs records report the value exported by each firm, product, destination
and year. The ownership data identify whether a firm belongs to a multinational group, the country
of its ultimate parent, and the size of the group's global network. Linking the two sources
requires matching tens of thousands of exporters to corporate records. We do so through a sequence
of procedures combining tax identifiers, company-name matching, validation with a large language
model, and manual review of the largest exporters. The resulting database classifies each exporter
as a foreign multinational affiliate, a domestic multinational affiliate, or a local firm.

We document five main patterns. First, multinational affiliates account for 48 percent of the
agricultural exports of the nine countries: 39 percent through foreign affiliates and 9 percent
through domestic affiliates. This share remained between 47 and 50 percent in every year of the
sample. Second, the ultimate parents of foreign affiliates are concentrated in a small number of
countries. The United States accounts for 25 percent of foreign-affiliate exports and the
Netherlands for 10 percent, while groups whose parents are recorded in Switzerland, Liechtenstein
and other tax havens account for 29 percent. Third, a small number of large groups dominate:
groups with more than 100 affiliates worldwide represent 13 percent of parents but account for
half of foreign-affiliate exports. Fourth, exports are higher in origin--destination--product
markets with more multinational firms, including exports by firms outside multinational groups.
Fifth, exports of multinational affiliates fall less with distance than exports of local firms,
especially when the group has an affiliate in the destination country. These are descriptive
patterns: the regressions identify conditional correlations, not causal effects of multinational
presence.

Our study contributes to three strands of the literature. First, a large body of work studies how
firms take part in international trade and why some of them become multinationals (see, e.g.,
Helpman, Melitz and Yeaple, 2004; Antr\`as and Yeaple, 2014; Bernard et al., 2018). This work shows
that the most productive firms invest abroad, that a few large firms account for most trade, and
that much multinational activity does not involve shipping goods within the group: in US
multinationals, the median affiliate ships nothing to the rest of its group (Ramondo, Rappoport and
Ruhl, 2016). Most of this evidence comes from manufacturing and from high-income countries. We
provide evidence on agriculture in nine Latin American countries, using customs records that cover
every exporter.

Second, we add to the work on multinational firms in agricultural value chains. This literature
shows that, in developing countries, agrifood chains have been reorganized around large
processors, traders and retailers that coordinate production with farmers through contracts and
standards (Swinnen and Maertens, 2007; Reardon et al., 2009; UNCTAD, 2009), and that a few large
trading companies handle much of world grain trade (Murphy, Burch and Clapp, 2012; Clapp, 2015).
Most of these studies focus on specific chains or companies. We measure the share of multinational
firms in all agricultural exports, by country, product and destination, and identify the countries
where their parents are based.

Third, we relate to the work on foreign direct investment and the exports of host countries. Plants
in Mexico are more likely to export when multinational exporters are nearby (Aitken, Hanson and
Harrison, 1997), and Chinese firms are more likely to start exporting a product to a country when
nearby foreign firms already export that product to that country (Mayneris and Poncet, 2015).
Foreign investment raises the productivity of domestic firms in the industries that supply
multinationals (Javorcik, 2004), domestic firms in Costa Rica that start supplying a multinational
grow and become more productive (Alfaro-Ure\~na, Manelici and Vasquez, 2022), and in Uruguay those
that start selling to a multinational are more likely to start exporting, especially to the
countries where that multinational has its headquarters or an affiliate (Carballo et al., 2021).
Our finding that firms outside multinational groups export more in the product--destination pairs
where multinationals are present is in line with this evidence. On distance, gravity equations
consistently find that trade falls with the distance between countries (Head and Mayer, 2014). Part of
this cost is fixed, such as finding buyers and setting up distribution, and fixed costs shape which
firms export to each market (Chaney, 2008); search costs also rise with distance and matter more
for differentiated goods (Rauch, 1999). Multinational firms face these costs too: the costs of
moving knowledge within the firm make the sales of affiliates fall with distance from the parent
(Keller and Yeaple, 2013), and firms often export to a market before investing there (Conconi,
Sapir and Zanardi, 2016). We look at a different margin: how distance affects the exports of
affiliates located in Latin America, and whether it matters less when the group is already present
in the destination.

Our results are also relevant for the policy discussion on how to attract foreign investment.
Investment promotion agencies help countries attract multinational firms by reducing information
barriers (Harding and Javorcik, 2011; Volpe Martincus et al., 2021). In Costa Rica, support from the
national agency raised the probability that a multinational firm opened its first affiliate in the
country (Carballo, Marra de Arti\~nano and Volpe Martincus, 2026). Our data show how important
these firms already are for agricultural exports. They also show that a large part of foreign
ownership is recorded in tax havens, in line with evidence that a large share of global foreign
direct investment and of multinational profits is booked in such jurisdictions (Damgaard, Elkjaer
and Johannesen, 2024; T{\o}rsl{\o}v, Wier and Zucman, 2023).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data,
the construction of the multinational database, and the main features of agricultural exports.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results. It first documents who owns these exports,
including the location of ultimate parents and the size of multinational groups, and then examines
how multinational presence is associated with export performance and whether distance is a smaller
barrier for multinational affiliates. Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes and discusses the policy implications.

\section{Data and Descriptive Statistics}\label{sec:data}
%======================================================================
\subsection{Data}\label{sec:data_data}

We rely on two main sources of data. The first one consists of export transactions from the
customs agencies of Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, El Salvador,
Paraguay, Peru and Uruguay. For each export, these data report the tax identifier and name of the
exporting firm, the product, the country of destination, the year and the value in US dollars
(FOB). Products are defined at the six-digit level of the Harmonized System (2007 revision). We use 2012--2018, the period for which all nine countries are observed, and focus on agricultural
products, defined as chapters 01 to 24 of the Harmonized System.

The second source consists of ownership data from two corporate databases: Orbis, compiled by
Bureau van Dijk, and the 2025 release of Dun \& Bradstreet. Customs records do not identify who
owns the exporting firm, so we use these sources to build a new database of multinational firms.
Its construction involves three stages.

In the first stage, we assemble the ownership information. From Orbis, we take for each company
its global ultimate owner, defined with ownership thresholds of 25 and 50 percent, the country of
the company and of its owner, and up to three national identifiers such as tax numbers, which links
about 17 million companies to about 13 million ultimate owners; Dun \& Bradstreet provides similar
information, including the name and country of each company's global ultimate parent. In the
second stage, we combine the two sources, since each covers firms that the other misses, by
matching company names across them, with an artificial intelligence agent reviewing each
candidate pair. In the third stage, we link the exporters in the customs data to the combined file
in four sequential steps, each applied to the exporters left unmatched by the previous one. We
first match tax identifiers. We then match names within the same country using the TF-IDF cosine
similarity of three-character sequences, keeping pairs with a similarity of at least 0.6 that a
large language model, using web search when needed, confirms as the same company or group. Next, we
repeat this name match against companies in all countries. Finally, we manually review the 500
largest unmatched exporters in each country, using company websites and national business
registries.

For matched firms whose parent country is missing, we recover it from the raw ownership links in
Orbis and an additional review with the language model. As a result, the country of the parent is
known for 89 percent of the agricultural exports of foreign affiliates. Combining the two
corporate databases proves important: about half of the matched agricultural export value
corresponds to firms found only in Orbis, a quarter to firms found only in Dun \& Bradstreet, and
the remaining quarter to firms found in both.

Exporters linked to the ownership file are classified as affiliates of multinational enterprises
(MNEs). An affiliate is classified as a \emph{domestic MNE} when its ultimate parent is located in
the exporting country and as a \emph{foreign MNE} otherwise; matched exporters without a recorded
parent country are classified as foreign. Exporters that cannot be linked to the file are
classified as \emph{local} firms, which we also refer to as non-MNE firms. Because the matching
procedure relies on ownership links, a group's ultimate parent exporting from its own country
cannot be identified as an affiliate and is therefore counted as a local firm.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}\label{sec:data_desc}

The final sample comprises 30,858 agricultural exporters, 6,330 of which are multinational
affiliates. Together they export 981 products to 242 destinations, for an average of US\$78.2
billion a year, and about 13,000 firms export agricultural products in an average year. Agriculture
accounts for 35 percent of the merchandise exports of the nine countries, but its weight differs
widely across them, from 86 percent in Paraguay, 62 percent in Uruguay and 57 percent in Argentina
to between 15 and 23 percent in Colombia, Peru, El Salvador, Chile and the Dominican Republic.
Argentina alone ships 48 percent of the region's agricultural exports.

Behind these aggregates are firms of very different size. Multinational affiliates are a fifth of
agricultural exporters, but they are larger and sell to more markets than local firms. Foreign
affiliates are 17 percent of exporters and ship 39 percent of the value, while local firms are 80
percent of exporters and ship 52 percent. In an average year, a foreign or a domestic affiliate
exports about US\$11.5 million, almost three times as much as a local firm (US\$4.2 million).
Affiliates also reach more destinations, 5.3 for foreign and 6.0 for domestic affiliates on
average against 3.8 for local firms, while the number of products per firm is similar in the
three groups (see Table~\ref{tab:ap_firms} in the Appendix). This pattern is consistent with the possibility that group membership lowers the cost of finding
buyers and entering additional markets.

Agricultural exports are concentrated in a small number of product groups. Animal feed (US\$14.1 billion a

dairy (23 percent), sugar (18 percent) and coffee (16 percent) (see Figure~\ref{fig:ap_sitc}). The
leading parent countries also change from one group to another: US groups lead in oil seeds (54
percent of the exports of foreign affiliates) and cereals (33 percent), Swiss and Liechtenstein
groups in animal feed (28 and 19 percent), German groups in coffee (42 percent), Saudi and

Finally, the presence of multinationals does not follow a clear pattern with product
sophistication. The foreign share rises and falls across groups of products ranked by the Product
Complexity Index, and it is similar for differentiated goods and for goods traded on organized
exchanges (43 and 41 percent). By end use, it is 42 percent for intermediate goods and 36 percent
for consumer goods (see Appendix~\ref{sec:ap_soph}).

\subsection{Ownership of Agricultural Exports}\label{sec:owners}

\subsubsection{Multinationals Account for Nearly Half of Agricultural Exports}

Multinational affiliates account for 48 percent of the agricultural exports of the nine countries:
39 percent through foreign affiliates and 9 percent through domestic affiliates (Figure~\ref{fig:f1}).
The share is highest in Paraguay (66 percent) and Uruguay (63 percent), where agriculture accounts
for the largest shares of merchandise exports, and lowest in Peru (38 percent) and El Salvador
(30 percent). Foreign affiliates account for a larger share than domestic affiliates in every
country. The gap is smallest in Chile (30 against 18 percent) and Colombia (28 against 15 percent),
where domestic multinational groups have a comparatively larger presence.

\subsubsection{Ultimate Parents Are Concentrated in a Few Countries}

The ultimate parents of foreign affiliates are concentrated in a small number of countries, and
more than a quarter of foreign-affiliate export value is associated with groups whose parents are
recorded in tax havens or similar jurisdictions. Among countries that are not classified as tax
havens, the United States accounts for 25 percent of foreign-affiliate exports and the Netherlands
for 10 percent; no other country reaches 5 percent (Figure~\ref{fig:parents}, Panel A). Tax havens
and similar jurisdictions account for 29 percent together, led by Switzerland (11 percent),
Liechtenstein (8 percent), Luxembourg (3 percent), and Panama (2 percent) (Panel B). Almost all of
the Swiss and Liechtenstein shares are accounted for by exporters in Argentina. Multinational groups
may hold foreign affiliates through entities in these jurisdictions, so the recorded parent country
does not necessarily identify where the group's operating activity takes place (Damgaard, Elkjaer
and Johannesen, 2024). Tables~\ref{tab:ap_pxd} and~\ref{tab:ap_pxp} in the Appendix report the
destinations of exports by parent country.

\subsubsection{A Few Large Groups Account for Most Foreign-Affiliate Exports}

Foreign-affiliate exports are concentrated among groups with large global networks. Although 44
percent of parents have only one affiliate worldwide, the 13 percent with more than 100 affiliates
account for half of foreign-affiliate exports (Figure~\ref{fig:network}). Groups with 11 to 100
affiliates account for another 30 percent, so groups with more than ten affiliates account for 80
percent of the total. Large networks may provide access to buyers, logistics, and market
information across countries. The concentration of exports among these groups is consistent with
this mechanism, although the descriptive evidence does not establish that network size causes higher
exports.

\subsection{Multinationals and Export Performance}\label{sec:perf}

We next examine how multinational presence is associated with export performance. We consider two
dimensions: whether aggregate exports and exports by local firms are higher in markets where
multinationals are present, and whether the negative association between distance and exports is
smaller for multinational affiliates. Because the data do not provide exogenous variation in
multinational presence, the estimates are interpreted as conditional correlations rather than
causal effects.

\subsubsection{Multinational Presence}

Exports may be higher where multinational firms are present for several reasons. Their affiliates
may provide access to buyers, distribution networks, and information about foreign standards. Other
firms may learn which products sell in which markets, use common logistics, or supply multinational
firms. To examine these relationships, we estimate a regression at the origin-country ($o$),
destination-country ($d$), product ($p$), and year ($y$) level:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:presence}
\ln X_{odpy} = \beta \ln N_{odpy} + \delta_{odp} + \delta_{ody} + \varepsilon_{odpy},
\end{equation}
where $X_{odpy}$ is the value of product $p$ exported from country $o$ to country $d$ in year
$y$, and $N_{odpy}$ is the number of multinational firms that export it. We estimate the intensive
margin using observations with at least one multinational firm, with $\ln N_{odpy}$ as the main
regressor. We then estimate the extensive margin, replacing $\ln N_{odpy}$ with an indicator equal
to one when at least one multinational firm exports the product from $o$ to $d$ in year $y$. In
our most demanding specification, $\delta_{odp}$ and $\delta_{ody}$ are fixed effects for each
origin--destination--product and origin--destination--year, respectively. They absorb persistent
differences in bilateral trade for a given product and changes over time in conditions specific
to each origin--destination pair. Other specifications include fewer fixed effects. Standard
errors are clustered by origin--destination pair.

We estimate each specification for two dependent variables. Panel A uses total exports, which
include exports by multinational firms themselves. Panel B uses exports by non-MNE firms only and
therefore examines whether firms outside multinational groups export more in markets where
multinationals are present. A positive coefficient in Panel B would be consistent with spillovers
or linkages through buyer information, shared logistics, or supplier relationships (Aitken, Hanson
and Harrison, 1997; Mayneris and Poncet, 2015; Carballo et al., 2021); a negative coefficient would
be consistent with displacement of other exporters. These regressions do not identify a causal
effect of multinational presence, since firms may select into the products and markets in which
they operate.

The estimates show that exports are higher where more multinational firms are present, including
exports by non-MNE firms. In the specification with the most demanding fixed effects, a 10 percent
increase in the number of multinationals exporting a product from one country to a given
destination in a given year is associated with about 8 percent higher total exports. Having at
least one multinational present is associated with exports about 90 percent higher (Table~\ref{tab:t1},
column 3 for each margin). Non-MNE firms also export more in these markets: the estimated elasticity
is 0.37, and their exports are 27 percent higher when at least one multinational is present (Panel
B). This positive association is consistent with linkages or spillovers that benefit local
exporters, but it does not establish that multinational presence causes their exports to increase.

with the destination (Table~\ref{tab:ap_counts} in the Appendix).

\emph{Foreign and domestic multinationals.} The estimated association is stronger for foreign
multinationals than for domestic ones. With the most demanding fixed effects, a 10 percent increase
in the number of foreign affiliates is associated with about 4.6 percent higher exports, compared
with 2.4 percent for domestic affiliates (column 4, Panel A). The corresponding elasticities for
non-MNE exports are 0.34 for foreign multinationals and 0.24 for domestic multinationals (column 4,
Panel B). One possible explanation is that foreign groups have broader networks abroad and can
provide affiliates with access to more buyers and information about foreign markets.

\emph{Foreign multinationals, by destination.} The association is stronger for foreign affiliates
exporting to third-country destinations than for those exporting to the parent's country. Under
the most demanding fixed effects, the elasticity is 0.37 for other destinations and 0.24 for the
parent's country (column 6, Panel A); for non-MNE exports, the corresponding estimates are 0.49 and
0.35 (column 6, Panel B). This pattern is consistent with affiliates in the region serving
third-country markets through export-platform investment (Ekholm, Forslid and Markusen, 2007),
rather than primarily shipping to their parent, and with evidence that many affiliates ship little
or nothing to other firms in their group (Ramondo, Rappoport and Ruhl, 2016). Only 4.5 percent of
foreign-MNE agricultural exports go to the parent's country (Table~\ref{tab:ap_pres}). These
estimates use fewer observations because identification requires at least one affiliate of each
type exporting the same product from the same origin to the same destination in the same year, and
are therefore less precise.

\subsubsection{Distance}

Distance can increase both variable transport costs and the fixed costs of finding buyers,
learning about demand, and enforcing contracts in distant markets (Rauch, 1999; Chaney, 2008; Head
and Mayer, 2014). Multinational affiliates may be better able to manage these costs through their
connections with headquarters and other affiliates, which may already have information about buyers
and local market conditions. We therefore examine whether the negative association between distance
and exports is smaller for multinational affiliates than for local firms, particularly when the
group is already present in the destination. We estimate a regression at the firm ($f$), destination
country ($d$), product ($p$), and year ($y$) level:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dist}
\ln x_{fodpy} = \alpha \ln D_{od} + \gamma \left(M_{f} \times \ln D_{od}\right) + \delta_{oy} + \delta_{dy} + \delta_{p} + u_{fodpy},
\end{equation}
where $x_{fodpy}$ is the value of product $p$ exported by firm $f$, located in country $o$, to
country $d$ in year $y$. The subscript $o$ is shown for clarity: each firm exports from a single
country, so the firm identifies the origin. $D_{od}$ is the distance between the two countries,
from the CEPII gravity database, and $M_f$ equals one if the firm is a multinational affiliate.
The coefficient $\alpha$ measures the distance gradient for local firms, while $\gamma$ measures
how this gradient differs for multinational affiliates. The fixed effects $\delta_{oy}$,
$\delta_{dy}$, and $\delta_p$ absorb origin-year and destination-year changes and persistent
differences across products; alternative specifications use origin, destination, and year fixed
effects. We also distinguish foreign from domestic multinationals and examine whether the group is
present in the destination, either through an affiliate there or because the destination is the
parent's country. Standard errors are clustered by origin--destination pair. These estimates are
also conditional correlations rather than causal effects.

The estimates indicate that distance is less negatively associated with exports for multinational
affiliates than for local firms. For local firms, a 1 percent increase in distance is associated
with a 0.21--0.22 percent decline in exports (Table~\ref{tab:t2}). The estimated distance gradient
is about 15 percent smaller for multinationals and about 40 percent smaller when the group has an
affiliate in the destination or the destination is the parent's country (columns 3 and 4). This
pattern is consistent with a role for multinational networks in lowering the costs of reaching
distant markets, but it does not establish that group presence causes the difference.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
%======================================================================
This paper combines customs records for nine Latin American countries with ownership data from
Orbis and Dun \& Bradstreet to document the role of multinational firms in agricultural exports
over 2012--2018. Multinational affiliates account for nearly half of these exports: 39 percent
through foreign affiliates and 9 percent through domestic affiliates. Their share remained between
47 and 50 percent throughout the period, even as aggregate exports fluctuated with commodity
prices. Multinational affiliates make up about one fifth of agricultural exporters, but export
almost three times as much as local firms and reach more destination markets.

Ownership is concentrated among a small number of parent countries and large corporate groups.
Groups with more than 100 affiliates worldwide account for half of foreign-affiliate exports, while
more than a quarter of that export value is associated with groups whose ultimate parent is
recorded in a tax haven or similar jurisdiction. The regression results also show that exports are
higher where more multinational firms are present, including exports by firms outside multinational
groups. In addition, the negative association between distance and exports is smaller for
multinational affiliates, particularly when the group is already present in the destination. These
patterns are consistent with multinational networks being associated with broader market access,
but the analysis does not establish causal effects.

Although the results are descriptive, they suggest three implications for policy. First,
investment-promotion policies may also have implications for export performance, particularly when
they address information barriers that affect multinational firms' location decisions (Harding and
Javorcik, 2011; Volpe Martincus et al., 2021; Carballo, Marra de Arti\~nano and Volpe Martincus,
2026). Second, local firms account for about half of agricultural exports but are smaller and reach
fewer markets. Programs that help them supply multinational firms may provide a channel through
which they can expand: local firms that start supplying a multinational grow (Alfaro-Ure\~na,
Manelici and Vasquez, 2022) and are more likely to start exporting (Carballo et al., 2021).
Export-promotion programs can also help them reach new markets and products (Volpe Martincus and
Carballo, 2008). Third, the concentration of exports among a few large groups and the importance
of parent entities recorded in tax havens highlight the need for better information on the ultimate
ownership of exporting firms. Beneficial-ownership registries and information exchange among tax,
customs, and competition authorities could make it easier to identify these firms and monitor their
trade.
